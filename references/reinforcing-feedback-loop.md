# Reinforcing Feedback Loop

**Category:** Systems thinking
**Also known as:** Amplifying loop, compounding loop, positive feedback loop

## Purpose

Explain growth or decline that feeds back into more growth or decline. Identify
the closed causal structure behind virtuous cycles, vicious cycles, and other
compounding behavior.

## Use When

- Change accelerates rather than proceeding linearly.
- Success creates conditions for more success, or decline creates more decline.
- Adoption, reputation, learning, trust, debt, backlog, or contagion compounds.
- A small initial difference grows substantially over time.

## Avoid When

- The process seeks a target or resists change; use Balancing Feedback Loop.
- There is no closed path returning influence to the starting variable.
- Exponential language is being used without evidence of amplification.
- External limits dominate the period of interest.

## Inputs

- A behavior-over-time pattern showing acceleration or compounding.
- At least two variables in a proposed closed causal path.
- Link polarities, mechanisms, delays, and evidence.
- Relevant external drivers and balancing limits.

## Procedure

1. Identify the variable whose growth or decline appears to amplify.
2. Identify another variable changed by it and describe the direct mechanism.
3. Continue tracing causal links until the path returns to the starting variable.
4. Mark each link's polarity and delay.
5. Confirm that the loop reinforces the original direction of change. The label
   describes amplification, not whether the outcome is desirable.
6. Describe the expected behavior over time and the conditions under which the
   loop becomes active or dominant.
7. Identify external inputs and balancing loops that eventually constrain,
   reverse, or stabilize the process.
8. Locate leverage: strengthen a beneficial loop, weaken a harmful loop, or add
   a balancing safeguard.
9. Define observations that would support or falsify the loop hypothesis.

## Guiding Questions

- What output from one cycle becomes an input to the next?
- What mechanism makes the next change larger or more likely?
- Is this a complete closed loop or only a one-way chain?
- Does the loop amplify growth, decline, or either direction?
- What delay hides the compounding effect?
- What resource, target, or counterforce eventually limits the loop?
- Which link offers leverage without unacceptable side effects?

## Output Format

- **Behavior:** observed acceleration, compounding, or collapse.
- **Loop path:** variable, polarity, variable, mechanism, delay.
- **Direction:** virtuous, vicious, or context-dependent consequence.
- **Activation conditions:** when the loop becomes influential.
- **Limits:** interacting balancing loops or external constraints.
- **Leverage and monitoring:** proposed intervention and expected pattern.

## Worked Example

A knowledge community sees useful contributions attract more active members;
more active members create more useful contributions; and the richer knowledge
base attracts still more members. The loop can support growth, but moderation
capacity forms a balancing constraint. If quality declines, contribution value
falls and the same structure can reverse into a decline. The team invests in
onboarding contributors and scales moderation before the reinforcing loop
overwhelms quality.

## Common Pitfalls

- Assuming "positive feedback" means a desirable outcome.
- Calling any upward trend a reinforcing loop.
- Omitting the causal path that closes the loop.
- Ignoring limits, delays, and interacting balancing loops.
- Treating correlation between growing variables as mechanism.
- Strengthening a beneficial loop without checking who bears the costs.

## Useful Combinations

- **Connection Circles** — map and validate the closed causal path.
- **Balancing Feedback Loop** — identify limits and stabilizing responses.
- **Second-Order Thinking** — explore how the loop changes later consequences.
- **Iceberg Model** — connect compounding behavior to structures and beliefs.
