# Impact-Effort Matrix

**Category:** Decision making
**Also known as:** Impact-work matrix, value-effort matrix

## Purpose

Prioritize a portfolio of tasks, initiatives, or ideas by comparing expected
impact with the effort required to deliver them.

## Use When

- A backlog or idea list is larger than available capacity.
- Rough portfolio placement is more useful than detailed weighted scoring.
- The group needs to distinguish quick wins from major investments.
- Items share a meaningful definition of impact and effort.

## Avoid When

- Urgency and obligation matter more than impact and effort.
- Items have hard dependencies that make independent placement misleading.
- Consequences are high-stakes and estimates are too uncertain for a simple map.
- Multiple value dimensions cannot be collapsed into one impact judgment.

## Inputs

- A bounded set of initiatives at comparable granularity.
- A defined beneficiary, outcome, and time horizon for impact.
- An effort definition covering time, people, cost, complexity, and risk.
- Rough estimates and confidence levels from relevant contributors.

## Procedure

1. Define the scope, strategic objective, and planning horizon.
2. Define consistent low-to-high scales for impact and effort.
3. Estimate impact using outcome change, reach, strategic fit, and confidence.
4. Estimate total effort, including coordination, adoption, maintenance, and
   opportunity cost rather than build time alone.
5. Place each item in one quadrant:
   - high impact, low effort: quick wins;
   - high impact, high effort: major projects;
   - low impact, low effort: fill-ins;
   - low impact, high effort: avoid, reduce, or redesign.
6. Mark uncertain placements and dependencies.
7. Choose a balanced portfolio rather than blindly sorting quadrants.
8. Revisit estimates as evidence and capacity change.

## Guiding Questions

- Impact on whom, measured by what outcome, over what period?
- Is this impact incremental or merely visible?
- What adoption or behavior change is required for impact to occur?
- Does effort include coordination, migration, maintenance, and risk?
- Which estimate is least reliable?
- Can a major project be reduced to a test or staged investment?
- Which quick win creates leverage for later work?

## Output Format

| Initiative | Impact | Effort | Confidence | Quadrant | Dependencies | Decision |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |

Add a short portfolio rationale: do now, plan, opportunistic, avoid or redesign,
and what evidence would change each placement.

## Worked Example

A product team maps four onboarding improvements. Fixing a broken verification
email is high impact and low effort, so it becomes a quick win. Rebuilding the
entire tutorial is high impact and high effort, so it is staged behind an
instrumented prototype. Renaming several labels is low impact and low effort,
so it remains a fill-in. A custom animation is low impact and high effort, so it
is removed. The team marks tutorial impact as uncertain and schedules a user
test before committing the full effort.

## Common Pitfalls

- Equating stakeholder enthusiasm with impact.
- Ignoring confidence and presenting rough placement as fact.
- Underestimating integration, coordination, and maintenance effort.
- Comparing tasks with projects at radically different granularity.
- Treating all quick wins as higher priority than strategic major projects.
- Ignoring dependencies, obligations, and portfolio balance.

## Useful Combinations

- **Eisenhower Matrix** — add urgency and importance to operational work.
- **Decision Matrix** — evaluate the short list with multiple criteria.
- **First Principles** — redesign a high-effort item around essential outcomes.
- **OODA Loop** — revisit the map as new delivery evidence arrives.
