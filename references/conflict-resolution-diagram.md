## Purpose

Resolve an apparent either-or conflict by separating opposing demands from the
needs they serve, identifying a shared objective, and challenging the assumptions
that make the demands appear mutually exclusive.

## Use When

- Two demands or positions appear incompatible.
- Both sides have legitimate needs that should not be traded away casually.
- The conflict is stalled by repeated advocacy or compromise.
- An internal dilemma can be expressed as two opposing actions.

## Avoid When

- Abuse, coercion, discrimination, or immediate safety concerns require
  protection and accountability rather than mutual problem solving.
- One demand violates a non-negotiable legal or ethical boundary.
- Parties lack authority or willingness to examine assumptions.
- Scarcity is truly fixed and distribution rules, not hidden assumptions, must
  decide the outcome.

## Inputs

- Demand D and opposing demand D-prime.
- Need B served by D and need C served by D-prime.
- Shared objective A that requires both needs to be met.
- Assumptions behind each necessity link and behind the apparent conflict.

## Procedure

1. Write the two concrete, opposing demands on the right side of the diagram.
2. Work backward and identify the legitimate need served by each demand.
3. Identify a shared objective that benefits from satisfying both needs.
4. Read the logic left to right:
   - to achieve A, we need B;
   - to achieve A, we need C;
   - to satisfy B, we must do D;
   - to satisfy C, we must do D-prime;
   - D and D-prime cannot coexist.
5. Surface assumptions behind every arrow and behind mutual exclusivity.
6. Test each assumption for necessity, current evidence, scope, and timing.
7. Generate an "injection": a new condition or action that breaks at least one
   assumption while preserving both needs and the shared objective.
8. Check the proposal with both sides, define risks, and run a bounded test when
   possible.

## Guiding Questions

- What does each side explicitly demand?
- What legitimate need would that demand satisfy?
- What shared objective makes both needs necessary?
- Why do we believe this demand is the only way to meet its need?
- Why do we believe the demands cannot coexist across time, scope, or segment?
- Which assumption is weakest, outdated, or testable?
- What new option satisfies both needs without a shallow compromise?

## Worked Example

Marketing demands a full website redesign to improve conversion, while finance
demands no redesign to avoid a large uncertain expense. Marketing's need is
better customer response; finance's need is capital discipline. The shared goal
is profitable growth. The conflict relies on assumptions that conversion gains
require a full redesign and that any redesign requires one large commitment.
The team breaks both assumptions with targeted page experiments and a staged
budget tied to measured improvement.

## Common Pitfalls

- Writing vague values instead of concrete demands and needs.
- Choosing a shared objective so abstract that it provides no constraint.
- Jumping to compromise before testing necessity assumptions.
- Treating one side's stated demand as its underlying need.
- Searching only for assumptions held by the other side.
- Declaring "win-win" without checking implementation cost and power dynamics.

## Useful Combinations

- **Ladder of Inference** — inspect assumptions and interpretations behind each
  position.
- **Abstraction Laddering** — find a useful level for the shared objective.
- **Zwicky Box** — generate injections across independent design dimensions.
- **Decision Matrix** — compare feasible injections after the conflict dissolves.
