# Iceberg Model

**Category:** Systems thinking
**Also known as:** Events-patterns-structures-mental-models framework

## Purpose

Move beneath a visible event to examine recurring patterns, the system
structures that produce them, and the beliefs or assumptions that sustain those
structures. Shift attention from reaction toward leverage.

## Use When

- The same type of event keeps recurring despite local fixes.
- A symptom appears across teams, periods, or contexts.
- Policies, incentives, information flows, or beliefs may shape behavior.
- The user wants prevention rather than another event-level response.

## Avoid When

- Immediate containment is required before deeper inquiry.
- There is only one isolated event and no evidence of a pattern.
- Hidden structures or mental models would be invented without research.
- A detailed causal-loop map or quantitative system model is already required.

## Inputs

- A clearly described event and immediate context.
- Historical observations or time-series evidence for patterns.
- Information about processes, incentives, rules, relationships, delays, and
  resource flows.
- Stakeholder perspectives on values, beliefs, goals, and assumptions.

## Procedure

1. **Events:** describe what happened without embedding a cause.
2. **Patterns:** look across time and cases. Identify frequency, trend,
   exceptions, cycles, and distribution rather than relying on one story.
3. **Structures:** identify arrangements that make the pattern likely: rules,
   incentives, capacity, roles, information access, feedback, delays, and
   dependencies.
4. **Mental models:** investigate beliefs, goals, norms, and assumptions that
   justify or reproduce the structures. Attribute them only with evidence.
5. Draw explicit links from mental models to structures, structures to patterns,
   and patterns to events.
6. Identify interventions at each level and compare their leverage, delay,
   feasibility, and unintended effects.
7. Select an intervention plus indicators that show whether the deeper pattern
   is changing.

## Guiding Questions

- What exactly happened?
- What has happened over time, and what are the exceptions?
- Which rules, incentives, capacities, delays, or relationships make this
  pattern predictable?
- What goals or beliefs make those structures seem reasonable?
- Whose mental model is being described, and what evidence supports it?
- Which intervention changes the conditions rather than only the event?
- How long would a deeper intervention take to affect visible outcomes?

## Worked Example

A product release contains several defects. Historical data shows defects after
most major releases, establishing a pattern. The structure includes fixed launch
dates, testing late in the cycle, and metrics that reward feature delivery but
not escaped defects. Interviews reveal a belief that raising quality concerns
signals low commitment to speed. The team still fixes the current defects, but
also changes release criteria, brings testing earlier, and tracks defect escape
rate across several cycles.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating a single event as proof of a pattern.
- Naming individual attitudes while ignoring organizational incentives.
- Guessing mental models and presenting them as facts.
- Assuming deeper always means more practical or more ethical.
- Skipping event containment because a structural fix sounds more strategic.
- Selecting an intervention without allowing for delay before results appear.

## Useful Combinations

- **Ishikawa Diagram** — expand possible causes within a structural area.
- **Connection Circles** — map relationships and feedback among structures.
- **Ladder of Inference** — test claims about stakeholder mental models.
- **OODA Loop** — monitor whether an intervention changes the pattern.
