# Concept Map

**Category:** Systems thinking
**Also known as:** Knowledge map, proposition map

## Purpose

Represent understanding of a concept or domain as explicit propositions: concept
nodes connected by meaningful linking phrases. Reveal hierarchy, cross-links,
misconceptions, and knowledge gaps.

## Use When

- A person or group needs to understand how concepts relate.
- Knowledge from several sources must be organized and communicated.
- Different stakeholder mental representations need comparison.
- A domain, product, process, or theory contains many interacting concepts.

## Avoid When

- The primary relationships are causal changes between variables; use Connection
  Circles.
- The task is a sequential workflow better represented by a process map.
- The mapper has too little domain knowledge and cannot verify propositions.
- A decorative mind map without explicit relationship labels would be enough.

## Inputs

- A focus question that gives the map purpose and boundary.
- Relevant domain knowledge and access to clarification where gaps exist.
- A list of important concepts, often roughly fifteen to twenty-five for a
  moderate map.
- Definitions and relationship language appropriate to the domain.

## Procedure

1. Write a focus question, such as "How does this system work for this user?"
2. Brainstorm important concepts relevant to the question.
3. Remove duplicates and separate concepts from examples, attributes, and
   linking phrases.
4. Sort concepts from general or inclusive to specific.
5. Arrange a provisional hierarchy with the broadest relevant concepts near the
   top.
6. Connect concepts with directional lines and concise linking words so each
   concept-link-concept path reads as a meaningful proposition.
7. Add cross-links between different branches where they reveal an important
   relationship.
8. Test every proposition for clarity and accuracy. Mark disputed or uncertain
   links.
9. Reorganize the map as understanding improves and record knowledge gaps.

## Guiding Questions

- What focus question should this map answer?
- Which items are concepts, and which are examples or properties?
- Can two connected nodes be read as a clear proposition?
- Is the link directional, and does the linking phrase say enough?
- Which cross-link produces a new insight?
- Where do participants disagree about a relationship?
- What missing concept or evidence prevents a complete answer?

## Output Format

- **Focus question:** one sentence.
- **Concept inventory:** normalized terms and definitions.
- **Propositions:** source concept, linking phrase, target concept, confidence.
- **Hierarchy:** general-to-specific structure.
- **Cross-links:** important relationships across branches.
- **Gaps or disputes:** unresolved terms, propositions, and next research.

## Worked Example

A new employee maps access control in a software product. The focus question is
"How does a document become visible to a user?" Concepts include organization,
workspace, role, membership, document, permission, link, and policy. Labeled
connections form propositions such as "membership grants role" and "policy
limits permission." A cross-link shows that public links bypass membership but
remain constrained by document policy. The map reveals an unresolved question
about inherited permissions that becomes a targeted investigation.

## Common Pitfalls

- Drawing unlabeled lines that do not express propositions.
- Mixing causal, temporal, spatial, and membership relations without clear
  linking phrases.
- Treating visual proximity as a defined relationship.
- Creating a hierarchy that reflects layout preference rather than meaning.
- Mapping beyond the focus question until the diagram becomes unbounded.
- Hiding uncertainty instead of marking disputed or missing knowledge.

## Useful Combinations

- **Abstraction Laddering** — refine the focus question and concept levels.
- **Issue Trees** — turn a knowledge gap into an analysis plan.
- **Connection Circles** — convert relevant concepts into causal variables when
  system behavior is the question.
- **Minto Pyramid** — communicate the map's main conclusion to an audience.
