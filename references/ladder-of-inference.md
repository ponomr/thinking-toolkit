## Purpose

Make the path from observable data to action visible so conclusions can be
checked for selective attention, interpretation, assumptions, and reinforcing
beliefs.

## Use When

- A strong conclusion rests on a small set of observations.
- People disagree about what an event means.
- Feedback, performance, or relationship judgments feel emotionally charged.
- A team is repeating a belief that filters what evidence it notices.

## Avoid When

- Immediate safety action is required before reflection.
- The dispute is primarily about values or interests rather than evidence.
- The user lacks access to any relevant observations.
- The framework could be used to dismiss lived experience instead of examining
  claims fairly.

## Inputs

- The action, judgment, or belief to examine.
- Available observable data, including what was not selected initially.
- The meanings, assumptions, and conclusions currently attached to the data.
- Alternative perspectives or sources of evidence.

## Procedure

1. Write the seven rungs from bottom to top:
   - available data;
   - selected data;
   - interpreted meaning;
   - assumptions;
   - conclusions;
   - beliefs;
   - actions.
2. Start at the current action, belief, or conclusion.
3. Descend one rung at a time and make each link explicit.
4. Return to available data. Add observations that were filtered out and
   distinguish direct observation from interpretation.
5. Generate at least two plausible alternative meanings or assumptions.
6. Identify what evidence would discriminate between interpretations.
7. Rebuild the ladder upward using the fuller evidence set.
8. Choose a proportionate action, often a question or test before a final
   judgment.

## Guiding Questions

- What action am I preparing to take, and what belief supports it?
- What exact conclusion did I draw?
- Which assumptions connect the observation to that conclusion?
- What did I directly observe, without adjectives or inferred motive?
- What data did I select, and what might I have ignored?
- What else could the same observation mean?
- What would I ask or observe to test competing interpretations?

| Rung | Current account | Evidence or basis | Alternative | Confidence |

End with a revised conclusion and the smallest fair next action.

## Worked Example

A manager thinks an engineer is disengaged because the engineer spoke only once
in two planning meetings. Descending the ladder reveals selected data about
speaking frequency, an interpretation that silence means low interest, and an
assumption that engaged people speak spontaneously. Missing data include written
design comments and whether the meeting format supports reflection. The manager
replaces a performance judgment with a private question about participation
preferences and checks contribution across channels.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating an interpretation as observable data.
- Using the ladder to prove another person is irrational.
- Generating alternatives but never gathering discriminating evidence.
- Assuming every conclusion is wrong merely because inference is involved.
- Ignoring the feedback loop in which beliefs influence future data selection.
- Turning reflection into delay when a reversible question would resolve it.

## Useful Combinations

- **Situation-Behavior-Impact** — turn verified observations into fair feedback.
- **Six Thinking Hats** — surface alternative perspectives without debate.
- **Inversion** — seek observations that would disconfirm the current belief.
- **OODA Loop** — test a revised interpretation through a small action.
- **`/logic` analysis** — name the specific fallacy when an inference outruns
  its evidence.
