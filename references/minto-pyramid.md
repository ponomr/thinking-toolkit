## Purpose

Make a message efficient and clear by leading with the conclusion, supporting it
with a small set of key arguments, and placing detailed evidence beneath the
argument it supports.

## Use When

- A busy audience needs the main point quickly.
- A recommendation, update, memo, or presentation has a defensible conclusion.
- Supporting evidence is too detailed to present in discovery order.
- The author needs a hierarchy that readers can skim or expand.

## Avoid When

- The conclusion is genuinely unknown and the task is exploratory dialogue.
- Safety or legal communication requires a mandated structure.
- A narrative experience is itself the communication goal.
- Leading with a conclusion would conceal uncertainty or prejudge consultation.

## Inputs

- The audience, decision, desired response, and context they already know.
- A clear conclusion, recommendation, or main takeaway.
- Two to five distinct supporting points.
- Evidence, analysis, examples, and caveats for each point.

## Procedure

1. Define what the audience should know, decide, or do after the message.
2. Write the conclusion first in one or two sentences. Include the requested
   action when appropriate.
3. Group the strongest supporting ideas into a small set of key points that are
   at the same logical level and together justify the conclusion.
4. Order the points by logic: deduction, chronology, structure, priority, or
   another explicit pattern.
5. Place facts, numbers, examples, and analysis beneath the point they support.
6. Remove material that does not change understanding or action.
7. Surface uncertainty, conditions, and counterevidence near the claim they
   qualify rather than burying them.
8. Test the vertical logic: each lower level answers "why" or "how" for the
   level above. Test horizontal logic: sibling points form a coherent group.
9. Adjust detail to the audience while preserving the same top-level message.

## Guiding Questions

- What is the one conclusion the audience needs first?
- What action or decision follows from it?
- Which key points are necessary and sufficient to support it?
- Are sibling points distinct and at the same level?
- What evidence belongs under each point?
- Which uncertainty or condition could change the recommendation?
- Can a reader stop after the first paragraph and still understand the message?

## Output Format

```text
Conclusion or recommendation
├── Key point 1
│   ├── Evidence
│   └── Evidence or caveat
├── Key point 2
│   ├── Evidence
│   └── Evidence or caveat
└── Key point 3
    ├── Evidence
    └── Evidence or caveat
```

Include requested action, owner or deadline, and conditions that trigger review.

## Worked Example

Instead of narrating six weeks of research, an analyst opens with: "Run a
three-month regional pilot rather than a national launch." Three supporting
points follow: demand is promising but uneven, operations can support one region
without new hiring, and a pilot resolves the largest pricing uncertainty. Each
point contains its evidence and caveat. Executives can act on the first paragraph
and inspect details only where needed.

## Common Pitfalls

- Leading with a topic rather than a conclusion.
- Using headings that are labels instead of claims.
- Mixing evidence, recommendations, and chronology at one level.
- Creating overlapping supporting points.
- Burying decisive uncertainty in an appendix or final paragraph.
- Forcing a confident answer when the evidence supports only a conditional one.

## Useful Combinations

- **Issue Trees** — turn a completed analytical structure into supporting points.
- **Decision Matrix** — communicate a recommendation and its decisive criteria.
- **Situation-Behavior-Impact** — structure the factual core of feedback.
- **Six Thinking Hats** — synthesize a multi-perspective discussion into action.
