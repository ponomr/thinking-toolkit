# Balancing Feedback Loop

**Category:** Systems thinking
**Also known as:** Stabilizing loop, goal-seeking loop, negative feedback loop

## Purpose

Explain behavior in which a system detects or experiences a gap from a target or
equilibrium and generates a counteracting response that reduces that gap.

## Use When

- A system resists change, stabilizes, oscillates, or returns toward a target.
- Corrective action grows when a discrepancy grows.
- Progress plateaus despite continued effort.
- A policy may be activating a hidden counterforce or delayed response.

## Avoid When

- Growth or decline is self-amplifying rather than goal-seeking.
- Variables and causal links have not been defined.
- "Balance" is being assumed beneficial without identifying whose goal it serves.
- A one-time opposing force is mistaken for a closed feedback process.

## Inputs

- A behavior-over-time pattern to explain.
- The desired level or equilibrium, whether explicit or implicit.
- The actual level, the gap, and the corrective action.
- Causal link polarities, delays, limits, and evidence.

## Procedure

1. Identify the variable whose behavior is stabilizing or approaching a target.
2. Identify the desired level or implicit goal. Ask who or what sets it.
3. Define the perceived gap between actual and desired level.
4. Identify the corrective action triggered by the gap.
5. Trace how the corrective action changes the actual level and therefore the
   future gap, closing the loop.
6. Mark link polarities and delays. Confirm that the closed loop counteracts the
   original change.
7. Explain expected behavior: convergence, overshoot, oscillation, resistance,
   or plateau.
8. Look for interacting reinforcing loops, capacity limits, distorted signals,
   and goals that shift over time.
9. Identify a leverage hypothesis and an indicator that could falsify the map.

## Guiding Questions

- What target or acceptable range is the system seeking?
- What is the actual level and how is the gap perceived?
- What action increases as the gap increases?
- What delay exists between action and effect?
- Does the response reduce the original discrepancy?
- Is the equilibrium desirable, imposed, or merely tolerated?
- Which other loop could dominate under different conditions?

## Output Format

- **Behavior:** observed stabilization, resistance, oscillation, or plateau.
- **Loop:** target, actual level, gap, corrective action, causal links, delays.
- **Goal owner:** actor, rule, or natural constraint setting the target.
- **Evidence and confidence:** per link.
- **Leverage hypothesis:** goal, signal, response, delay, or capacity change.
- **Monitoring:** expected pattern if the hypothesis is correct.

## Worked Example

A subscription service increases acquisition spending when new sign-ups fall
below target. More spending raises sign-ups after a delay, reducing the gap and
therefore future spending. The delay causes periodic overshoot: spending remains
high after sign-ups have begun recovering. The loop suggests that improving the
signal and shortening the measurement delay may reduce oscillation more safely
than simply lowering the acquisition target.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating balancing as morally good or operationally effective.
- Failing to identify the target or who controls it.
- Omitting perception and measurement errors in the gap.
- Ignoring delays that create overshoot and oscillation.
- Describing two opposing variables without closing the loop.
- Assuming one loop explains behavior across all time periods.

## Useful Combinations

- **Connection Circles** — find and document the complete closed path.
- **Reinforcing Feedback Loop** — analyze competing growth and limiting forces.
- **Iceberg Model** — connect the target and response to policies and beliefs.
- **OODA Loop** — improve sensing and corrective-action cadence.
