# Hard Choice Model

**Category:** Decision making
**Also known as:** Impact-comparability matrix, decision-type matrix

## Purpose

Calibrate the time, rigor, and method invested in a decision by considering its
impact and how easily its options can be compared.

## Use When

- The user is unsure whether a choice deserves quick judgment or deep analysis.
- Options differ in kind, not merely degree.
- High-stakes choices combine evidence with values or identity.
- Overthinking small decisions or underthinking major decisions is a risk.

## Avoid When

- The options or decision boundary are not yet defined.
- A mandatory rule or hard constraint determines the action.
- Immediate crisis containment is required.
- Impact and comparability labels would conceal major uncertainty rather than
  guide the next method.

## Inputs

- The decision, options, deadline, and decision owner.
- Impact across magnitude, duration, affected people, and reversibility.
- Comparability across shared criteria, evidence quality, and value conflict.
- Available ways to test, delay, stage, or reverse the choice.

## Procedure

1. Define impact as low or high relative to the decision owner's context.
2. Define comparability as easy or hard based on whether options share meaningful
   criteria and reliable evidence.
3. Place the decision in one quadrant:
   - low impact, easy comparison: no-brainer;
   - low impact, hard comparison: apples and oranges;
   - high impact, easy comparison: big choice;
   - high impact, hard comparison: hard choice.
4. Match the approach:
   - decide a no-brainer quickly with a simple rule;
   - use current preferences and a timebox for apples and oranges;
   - gather evidence and compare carefully for a big choice;
   - clarify values, identity, commitments, and experiments for a hard choice.
5. Identify whether staging, trial periods, or option preservation can reduce
   impact or improve comparability.
6. Set a decision deadline and stopping rule.

## Guiding Questions

- How large, durable, and reversible is the consequence?
- Who else is materially affected?
- Can the options be judged on shared criteria without distorting them?
- Is the difficulty caused by missing evidence or by competing values?
- What small experiment could make the options more comparable?
- What kind of person or organization do we commit to becoming through this
  choice?
- What is the cost of delaying the decision?

## Output Format

- **Decision:** one sentence.
- **Impact:** low or high, with reasons and reversibility.
- **Comparability:** easy or hard, with reasons.
- **Decision type:** one quadrant.
- **Approach:** timebox, evidence needed, values to clarify, and method.
- **Option-preserving move:** experiment, stage, trial, or exit condition.
- **Decision deadline:** explicit.

## Worked Example

A designer is choosing between a senior role at a stable company and an early
startup role. The impact is high and the options are hard to compare because
salary, learning, autonomy, risk, and identity do not reduce cleanly to one
scale. The model classifies it as a hard choice. Instead of seeking a fictional
objective winner, the designer clarifies values, interviews future teammates,
negotiates a short advisory project with the startup, and sets a decision date.

## Common Pitfalls

- Calling a choice hard merely because information has not been gathered.
- Treating low-impact decisions as deserving optimization.
- Forcing incommensurable values into false numerical precision.
- Assuming a hard choice has a hidden uniquely correct answer.
- Ignoring reversibility and ways to reshape the option set.
- Delaying indefinitely without a stopping rule.

## Useful Combinations

- **Decision Matrix** — analyze high-impact options that are meaningfully
  comparable.
- **First Principles** — challenge the assumption that the option set is fixed.
- **Second-Order Thinking** — examine durable consequences.
- **OODA Loop** — create learning through a reversible experiment.
