# Abstraction Laddering

**Category:** Problem solving
**Also known as:** Why-how ladder, problem-framing ladder

## Purpose

Reframe a problem by moving upward toward broader purposes with "why" and
downward toward concrete means with "how." Discover alternative frames and
solution paths that the initial statement obscures.

## Use When

- The initial problem statement feels narrow, solution-biased, or vague.
- A team is optimizing a means without agreeing on the desired outcome.
- Creative alternatives are blocked by the current level of abstraction.
- A broad ambition needs to be translated into actionable questions.

## Avoid When

- The problem is already specific, verified, and routine.
- The primary need is causal diagnosis rather than reframing.
- Repeated "why" questions could feel accusatory in a sensitive conversation;
  rephrase them as purpose questions.
- The group has no authority over the broader system reached by moving upward.

## Inputs

- An initial problem or solution statement.
- The stakeholder, outcome, scope, and constraints.
- Evidence about why the issue matters and what makes it difficult.
- A stopping boundary for frames outside the decision maker's influence.

## Procedure

1. Place the initial statement in the middle of a ladder.
2. Move upward by asking why the outcome matters or what broader purpose it
   serves. Write each answer as a new problem frame, not merely an explanation.
3. Repeat upward until another step becomes too broad, outside scope, or no
   longer decision-relevant.
4. Return to the initial statement and move downward by asking how the outcome
   could be achieved or what more specific problem must be solved.
5. Branch at each level rather than following only one path.
6. Inspect the ladder for a frame that is broad enough to permit alternatives
   but specific enough to guide action.
7. Restate the chosen frame with stakeholder, outcome, scope, and constraints.
8. Generate at least three solution directions from the chosen level.

## Guiding Questions

- Why does this matter, and to whom?
- What larger outcome would solving this enable?
- Are we treating a preferred solution as the problem?
- How else could the broader purpose be achieved?
- What concrete obstacle prevents the desired outcome?
- Which level is within our influence and measurable?
- What changes when we choose a different rung?

## Output Format

- **Initial statement:** original wording.
- **Upward ladder:** progressively broader purpose frames.
- **Downward branches:** progressively concrete means or subproblems.
- **Chosen frame:** stakeholder, outcome, scope, constraints.
- **Why this rung:** leverage, actionability, and evidence.
- **Alternative directions:** at least three distinct paths.

## Worked Example

A team starts with "build a better search filter." Moving upward yields "help
customers narrow a large catalog" and then "help customers reach a confident
choice quickly." Moving downward from that broader frame produces filters,
guided questions, comparison, curated collections, and improved result ranking.
The team chooses "reduce time to a confident choice for first-time buyers" as a
more useful frame and tests several approaches rather than redesigning filters
by default.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating each answer as the only possible next rung.
- Moving upward until the frame becomes inspirational but unactionable.
- Moving downward into implementation before validating the chosen purpose.
- Confusing "why did this happen" causal inquiry with "why does this matter"
  purpose inquiry.
- Ignoring authority, constraints, and the affected stakeholder.
- Selecting a novel frame only because it sounds creative.

## Useful Combinations

- **First Principles** — rebuild solutions from the chosen frame's fundamentals.
- **Issue Trees** — decompose the selected actionable frame.
- **Productive Thinking Model** — continue from framing into solution and action.
- **Concept Map** — clarify concepts within a broad or contested frame.
