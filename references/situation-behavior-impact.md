# Situation-Behavior-Impact

**Category:** Communication
**Also known as:** SBI feedback model, behavior-based feedback

## Purpose

Give specific, observable, and useful feedback by naming the context, describing
behavior without inferred motive, and explaining its impact. Create a foundation
for dialogue rather than a verdict about character.

## Use When

- Positive or corrective feedback must be concrete and actionable.
- Interpretation and judgment are making a conversation defensive.
- A manager, peer, or collaborator wants to discuss one observed behavior.
- The impact on the speaker, team, work, or customer can be described honestly.

## Avoid When

- Immediate safety, harassment, discrimination, or serious misconduct requires
  a formal process or protective action.
- The speaker did not observe the behavior and cannot verify the account.
- Feedback would expose a vulnerable person to retaliation.
- A pattern needs several examples and broader performance expectations, not one
  isolated incident.

## Inputs

- A specific time and context.
- Observable words or actions, separated from labels and inferred intent.
- A truthful impact on outcome, process, people, or the speaker.
- The desired dialogue, reflection, or future behavior.

## Procedure

1. Clarify the purpose of the feedback and choose an appropriate private or
   public setting.
2. **Situation:** identify when and where the behavior occurred with enough
   detail for shared recall.
3. **Behavior:** describe what the person said or did in neutral, observable
   language. Avoid motive, diagnosis, and character labels.
4. **Impact:** explain the effect on you, others, the work, or the outcome.
   Distinguish measured impact from your interpretation or feeling.
5. Pause and invite the recipient's perspective.
6. When useful, ask about intent without assuming it. Explore any gap between
   intent and impact.
7. Agree on reinforcement, change, support, or a follow-up observation.

## Guiding Questions

- What exact situation will the other person recognize?
- What would a camera or transcript have captured?
- Which words in my draft imply motive or character?
- What specific impact occurred, and how do I know?
- Am I describing my reaction as mine rather than universal fact?
- What perspective or intent have I not heard?
- What future behavior or support would be useful?

## Output Format

- **Purpose:** why this conversation matters.
- **Situation:** "During or when..."
- **Behavior:** "I observed or heard..."
- **Impact:** "The effect was..." or "I felt or concluded..."
- **Invitation:** "How did you see it? What was your intent?"
- **Next step:** reinforcement, request, support, and follow-up.

## Worked Example

"During Monday's release review, when you summarized the incident, you named the
engineering changes but did not mention the support team's customer analysis.
The support team then had to repeat the context, and two people told me their
work felt invisible. How did you decide what to include? For the next review, can
we use a shared outline and make each contributing team visible?"

## Common Pitfalls

- Describing interpretation as behavior, such as "you were dismissive."
- Using a vague situation or a collection of "always" and "never" claims.
- Exaggerating impact to make the feedback persuasive.
- Treating impact as proof of harmful intent.
- Delivering a polished monologue without listening.
- Ending without a clear request, reinforcement, or follow-up.

## Useful Combinations

- **Ladder of Inference** — remove unsupported interpretation before drafting.
- **Conflict Resolution Diagram** — address competing needs behind a persistent
  disagreement.
- **Minto Pyramid** — structure a longer written feedback or performance message.
- **OODA Loop** — observe and adjust after agreed behavior changes.
